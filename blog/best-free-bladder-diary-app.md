## What does free mean in a bladder diary app?

Four different deals go by the same word, and the App Store page tells you which one you are looking at before you download anything.

Some apps are free outright, with nothing to pay and nothing to unlock. Some are free because advertising pays for them, including at three in the morning, in a bathroom, half way through an entry. Some give you a trial of a week or two, after which recording stops or locks what you have already entered, part way through the consecutive days the clinic asked for. The most common arrangement is free to record and paid to get the diary out: logging costs nothing, and the report, the export or the charts sit behind a purchase.

Two lines on the App Store page settle which one you have: the In-App Purchases list, which names what is sold and whether it renews, and the privacy label. A diary is usually a job of a few days, so a subscription and a one-time purchase are not the same deal.

## What the diary has to record

The clinic's form has a column for how much you passed, in millilitres, and that is the column an app is most likely to leave out.

The European Association of Urology separates two documents in its guideline on male lower urinary tract symptoms. A frequency volume chart records the time and volume of each void; a bladder diary adds fluid intake, pad usage, activities and symptom scores, and the guideline asks for at least three days of it.[1] The International Continence Society describes the bladder diary the same way: voided volumes with times, plus incontinence episodes, pad usage and intake.[2] A review of the evidence on diary content, format and duration makes the same point: what the diary is for sets what it has to contain.[3]

An app that counts taps gives you a number of trips. That is one line of the chart the clinic asked for, and the measured volumes are the part nobody can reconstruct afterwards. So the first thing to look for in the screenshots is a numeric volume field, in millilitres or ounces, and a way to record a trip you could not measure. Without the second one people skip the entry altogether, and a missing record is worse than an approximate one.

## Seven checks before you install

- A volume on every trip, in millilitres or ounces, with a way to mark a trip you did not measure.
- The time attached automatically, and editable when you log half an hour late.
- Nights separated from days: a night marker, or bed and wake times.
- Three to seven days in one diary, because that is the range clinics ask for. The research comparing three-day and seven-day diaries found the shorter one gives comparable measurements with better compliance.[4]
- Somewhere for urgency, leaks and pads, as fields or at least as a note.
- An export a clinician can read: a printed page or a PDF, not a screenshot of a chart.
- The privacy label, and whether an account is required at all before the app will record anything.

Those seven take about two minutes on the App Store page, and none of them is what the best-app lists sort by.

## What free costs you later

The common failure comes on the last day: three days recorded, and then the report sits behind a payment you did not expect, or the entries will not leave the app in any form a clinician can read.

A 2026 framework analysis of electronic bladder diary apps found that most of them lacked important clinical items, had no professional involvement in their design, and were weak on the security of health information, and concluded that apps were not yet a match for validated paper diaries.[5]

The check costs nothing. On day one, before you have anything to lose, open the export or report screen and see what happens. If it asks for money, you still have the choice of another app and no entries to move.

## Is a free app enough for the appointment?

Yes, when it holds the time and volume of each trip, keeps the nights apart, covers the days you were asked for, and hands the result over in a form someone can read. Clinics ask for the diary because it is a record of what happened rather than what you remember,[6] and a free app with every trip in it beats a paid one with gaps on the second night.

No app should interpret the result. Your numbers mean something next to your history, and that reading belongs to your clinician.

## Questions

**Is there a completely free bladder diary app?**
Several apps let you record for nothing. What is usually paid for is the part you need at the end: the printable report, the export, or the charts. That is fair enough as long as you know on day one, so look for the In-App Purchases line on the App Store page and open the app's own pricing screen before you have three days of entries to lose.

**How can I tell what a free app does with my entries?**
Three places, all readable in a minute: the privacy label on the App Store page, which either says Data Not Collected or lists what is linked to you; whether the app demands an account before it will record anything; and the privacy policy. A 2026 framework analysis of bladder diary apps found most were weak on the security of health information.

**Will a urologist accept a diary kept in an app?**
Usually, if it shows what the clinic's own form shows: the time and volume of each trip, what you drank, nights separated from days, and the days you were asked to cover. Bring it printed or as a PDF, since some clinicians prefer paper on the desk. If the clinic gave you its own form with its own scales, fill in that form.
