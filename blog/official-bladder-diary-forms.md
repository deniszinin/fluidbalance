# Official Bladder Diary Forms: NHS, AUA, ICIQ and NIDDK
Source: https://fluidbalanceapp.com/blog/official-bladder-diary-forms/
Published: 2026-11-24 · Guide · By Denis Zinin, developer of Bladder Diary: Fluid Balance. Not a doctor.

In short:
Official bladder diary forms do not agree with each other, and the difference that matters most is whether the form asks you to write a measured number for each trip or only to tick a box. The sheet your clinic hands you is one of several, each issued by a different body, and knowing which one you are holding tells you what to prepare before day one:

- The NIDDK's Your Daily Bladder Diary asks for drinks as a number in ounces, millilitres or cups, but records how much urine as small, medium or large. European and French descriptions ask for the measured volume of every void.
- Durations differ: NICE asks for a minimum of 24 hours, the EAU for at least three days, the NIDDK for two to three days, and the ICIQ diary was developed and validated as a three-day document.
- If your clinic gave you its own sheet, fill in that sheet. The columns overlap enough that entries move across without rewriting.

There is no single official bladder diary. There are several, issued by different bodies in different countries, and they ask for different things. If a nurse handed you a sheet and you went looking online for the proper version, that is why the results disagree: you were comparing documents that were never meant to match.

The practical consequence is small but easy to get wrong. One form wants a number in millilitres for every trip. Another wants you to tick small, medium or large. If you prepare for the second and your clinic wanted the first, you have three days of entries with the column that mattered left blank.

## Which bladder diary form does the NHS use?

There isn't one NHS form — individual trusts publish their own, and the one we keep going back to is the frequency volume chart from Gloucestershire Hospitals.[8] It is worth looking at because of three details most sheets leave out: a tick for a trip you could not measure, separate lines for the time you went to bed and the time you woke, and a worked example filled in at the top.

That tick matters more than it looks. Without somewhere to record a trip you did not measure, people leave the row empty, and an empty row reads as a trip that never happened.

In guidance rather than paper, NICE asks men with bothersome lower urinary tract symptoms to complete a urinary frequency volume chart as part of the initial assessment, over a minimum of 24 hours.[6]

## The American forms, and the column they skip

The NIDDK — part of the National Institutes of Health — publishes a one-page sheet titled Your Daily Bladder Diary.[1] It is a fixed hourly grid, running 6–7 a.m. down to 5–6 a.m., with a sample row at the top. Its columns are: what you drank and how much, trips to the bathroom, accidental leaks, whether you felt a strong urge to go, and what you were doing at the time — the sheet suggests sneezing, lifting, arriving home, sleeping.

Here is the detail worth knowing before you print it. For drinks, the form asks "How much?" and offers oz, mL, cups — a number. For urine, both for trips and for leaks, it offers **sm, med, lg**. There is no numeric column for how much you passed. At the foot of the page it also asks how many pads and how many diapers you used that day, as a number.

The sheet says to use it as a master for copies, "for as many days as you need", and the NIDDK's diagnosis page suggests keeping a diary for two to three days before seeing a health care professional.[2]

The Urology Care Foundation, the American Urological Association's patient-facing arm, takes a different line again: free rows rather than a fixed hourly grid, container sizes offered as reference amounts, and days that need not be consecutive.[9]

## In France it is a calendrier mictionnel

French-speaking patients search for a *calendrier mictionnel*, and some hospitals print the same sheet as a *catalogue mictionnel*. A functional urology handbook published on the Association Française d'Urologie's site describes it as an extension of the clinical interview and sets out what has to be on the sheet: the times of voids, the time of going to bed, the time of getting up, the degree of urge, the presence of leakage, and the volume of the voids — with the patient collecting urine and measuring the volume of each void over 24 hours, two to three days running.[7]

Two things stand out. The volume of every void is required, not optional. And fluid intake is not in that list at all — which is the line between a frequency volume chart and a fuller bladder diary.

The same handbook goes on to what a clinician then calculates: the 24-hour output, the smallest and largest voided volumes, and the nocturnal polyuria index. It prints threshold figures alongside those. We have left the figures out on purpose, as we do everywhere on this site: your numbers mean something next to your history, and that reading belongs to your clinician.

## The ICIQ diary, and what validated means here

The European Association of Urology separates the two documents cleanly. A frequency volume chart records the time and volume of each void; a bladder diary adds fluid intake, pad usage, activities and symptom scores. It asks for at least three days of it, and notes that the ICIQ bladder diary is the only fully validated one.[3]

Validated is a specific claim, not a compliment. The ICIQ diary was developed and tested under the ICIQ psychometric protocol, and the work compared recording lengths before settling on three days; the three-day version was reported as valid, reliable and responsive to change, and was adopted as the ICIQ bladder diary.[4]

It is also the one form here you cannot simply download and ship. The ICIQ states that its questionnaires are copyright protected and that use requires permission for an approved purpose; its licence table makes routine clinical care, audit, service evaluation and non-commercial research free, and charges a licence fee for large-scale funded research and for commercial products and platforms.[5] That is why you will not find it inside most apps, including ours.

## How long each one asks for

| Source | What it calls the document | Duration it asks for |
|---|---|---|
| NICE (CG97, men) | Urinary frequency volume chart | Minimum 24 hours[6] |
| EAU (male LUTS) | Bladder diary | At least three days[3] |
| ICIQ | ICIQ bladder diary | Three days[4] |
| NIDDK | Your Daily Bladder Diary | Two to three days before the appointment[2] |
| AFU handbook | Calendrier mictionnel | 24 hours, two to three days running[7] |
| Urology Care Foundation | Bladder diary | Days need not be consecutive[9] |

The spread is narrower than it looks. Nobody asks for less than a day or more than a week, and the International Continence Society describes the same contents throughout: voided volumes with times, plus incontinence episodes, pad usage and intake.[10]

## If your clinic gave you a form, fill in that form

This is the short answer to most of the questions above. A clinic's own sheet may carry scales and wording specific to that service, and the person reading it reads that layout every week. Swapping in a nicer-looking form helps nobody.

What travels between all of them is the substance: the clock time of each trip, how much came out, what you drank, and which trips were at night. Get those four right and the sheet underneath them hardly matters.

## Questions

**Which bladder diary form should I use?**
The one your clinic gave you, if it gave you one — it is the sheet the person reading it is used to, and it may carry scales specific to that service. If nobody gave you a form, any sheet works as long as it holds the time and the volume of each trip, what you drank, and a mark for nights. That is the overlap between every form described here.

**Is the ICIQ bladder diary free to use?**
The ICIQ states that its questionnaires are copyright protected and that permission is granted for a specific approved purpose. Its own licence table lists routine clinical care, audit, service evaluation and non-commercial research as free, with a licence fee for large-scale funded research and for commercial products and platforms. So a clinic can use it at no cost, while an app that shipped it would be licensing it.

**Why does one form ask for millilitres and another just small, medium or large?**
Because they are built for different jobs. A tick or a size category is quicker and gets more sheets filled in, which matters when the question is a pattern of leaks. A measured volume is the only way to get the largest void and the night output, which is what the European guideline describes being calculated from a diary. If the sheet has a numeric column, filling it in is the part nobody can reconstruct afterwards.
