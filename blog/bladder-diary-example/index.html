Bladder diary example: one full day, line by line

28 September 2026· 5 min read

In short

A bladder diary example for one full day is set out below, line by line: fifteen or so entries with every drink and its amount, every trip with a volume or a not-measured tick, the bed and wake times, and a note where something happened. The day is made up, so its totals are not targets. The layout follows the Urology Care Foundation diary and the NHS charts, whose own forms show only one or two example rows. What the day is meant to show:

  • A full diary day is fifteen or so lines: every drink with its amount, every trip with a volume or a tick, the bed and wake times, and a note where something happened.
  • The example below is one made-up day. Its totals are not targets, and there is no normal to compare them with here.
  • A clinician reads it for the count of trips by day and night, the volumes, the drinks pattern, the notes, and whether the day is complete.

By Denis Zinin, developer of Bladder Diary: Fluid Balance. Not a doctor.

Clinic forms show one or two example lines: the Urology Care Foundation’s diary has a single row with a can of soda and a leak while laughing,1 the NHS Gloucestershire chart shows a morning with a cup of tea and a leak,2 the Sussex physiotherapy chart three morning lines with totals.3 Helpful, but nobody’s day is three lines long. Here is a whole made-up day in the format of our printable template, followed by what each part of it tells a clinician. The numbers are this example’s numbers and nothing else; there is no normal to compare them with on this page.

What does a full bladder diary day look like?

A filled-in bladder diary example. Day 1 of 7. Went to bed 22:45. Got up 7:00.

Time Drank (ml) What Passed (ml or ✓) Night Note
7:05 350 first of the morning
7:20 250 tea
8:30 200 coffee
9:40 ✓ in a hurry, small leak
11:00 500 water
11:45 220
13:10 330 juice
13:30 180 strong urge
15:00 250 tea
16:20 260
18:00 250 water
19:30 200 beer
20:15 300
22:30 150 before bed
3:10 180 ✓

Fifteen lines, one page. The day runs from getting up to getting up, so the 3:10 trip belongs to Day 1 even though the date has changed.

Line by line

7:05, 350 ml, “first of the morning”. The first trip after getting up. Not a night trip, because the diary’s night ended at 7:00, but by the ICS definition the urine passed then was made overnight and is added to the night-time volume,5 which is why it is measured rather than ticked. On paper it is simply the first line of the day.

7:20 and 8:30, tea and coffee. Drinks with the amount and the type. The amount comes from measuring the mug once; the type is there because clinic forms ask for it.1,2

9:40, a tick, “in a hurry, small leak”. No time to measure, so a tick instead of a volume, and the trip still counts towards the day’s total of trips.2 The note records the leak with a rough size. On a form with leak columns this would be a “small” or a “+”; on a plain diary it is a note with the time.

11:00, 500 ml of water. A bottle, so the amount is printed on it.

13:30, 180 ml, “strong urge”. A measured trip with urgency noted: a sudden, compelling need to go.4 Written at the time, in two words, which is all it needs.

19:30, 200 ml of beer. Alcohol is a drink like any other on the sheet: amount, type, time. The diary is meant to show a normal evening, not a tidied one.

22:30, 150 ml, “before bed”. The last trip before the bed time written at the top of the page. It is a daytime trip; the night starts at 22:45.

3:10, 180 ml, ticked Night. Between going to bed and getting up, so it gets the tick. This is the line that makes the night count possible; a diary without bed and wake times cannot tell this trip from the one at 22:30.

What this day adds up to

Only because the example allows it: 1,980 ml drunk across seven drinks; 1,640 ml passed across seven measured trips, plus one trip ticked without a volume; eight trips in all, one of them at night. On a clinic form you may be asked to write these totals in the bottom row; in the app they appear on the Today screen and in the report without any adding up. They are not targets and they are not typical of anyone in particular. Their only job here is to show what a complete day looks like when it is counted.

What a clinician sees in it

Eight trips with times, one at night, one unmeasured. Seven measured volumes, from 150 to 350 ml. Drinks spread from 7:20 to 19:30 with the types written down. Two notes, a leak with a trigger and an urge. Bed and wake times, so the night can be separated from the day. And a day that is complete from waking to waking, which is the first thing they check, because memory alone gets even the count of trips wrong.6 What those facts mean is the appointment; the diary’s job was to bring them.

Your day will look different

More lines or fewer, other drinks, other times. The format is what stays the same: every drink, every trip, a volume or a tick, the two times that frame the night, and a note where something happened. Questions

Where do I write the first trip of the morning?

As the first line of the new day, after your "got up" time, with its volume if you measured it. It is not a night trip, because you were up, but its volume counts as night-time urine by the ICS definition,5 so it is worth measuring. The clinician does the sorting.

Do I have to add up the totals?

No. Clinic forms leave a total row, and some ask for it, but the clinician can add it up; the NHS chart says as much.2 If you use the app, the totals, the trip counts and the night count are done for you. What you can't delegate is the lines themselves.

What if my day has twenty lines?

Use the next page and carry on. A day with many lines is a complete day, which is the only kind the clinician wants; leaving lines out to make it fit is the mistake. Our template has 24 lines a page on A4, and the app has no limit.
